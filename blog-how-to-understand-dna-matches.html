How to Understand DNA Matches and Find Which Side of the Family They Come From

Confused by DNA matches? Learn what match percentages mean, how to identify the maternal or paternal side, group relatives, and build a useful family tree.

Receiving a long list of DNA matches can feel exciting for about five minutes.

Then the confusion begins.

Who are these people? Why do some matches have familiar surnames while others appear completely unrelated? Which matches belong to your mother’s family, and which come from your father’s side?

A DNA match is not a finished family tree. It is a clue showing that you and another person appear to share inherited DNA.

To make that clue useful, you need to combine the match with family trees, shared relatives, surnames, locations, ages, and historical records.

This beginner-friendly guide explains how to understand DNA matches, organize them, and turn an overwhelming list of strangers into a practical family-research plan.

What Is a DNA Match?

A DNA match is another person in a testing company’s database who shares identifiable segments of DNA with you.

Generally, a larger amount of shared DNA suggests a closer biological relationship. However, one number alone does not always reveal the exact relationship.

The same approximate amount of shared DNA may fit several possibilities.

For example, a match could potentially be:

  • a first cousin;
  • a half-aunt or half-uncle;
  • a great-aunt or great-uncle;
  • a great-grandparent;
  • a great-grandchild.

Age, family trees and shared matches help distinguish between these possibilities.

What Does cM Mean in DNA Results?

Many DNA platforms display shared DNA in centimorgans, usually abbreviated as cM.

Centimorgans do not measure the physical length of DNA in centimeters. They represent the estimated likelihood that DNA segments will be inherited together.

For beginners, the practical rule is simple:

The more centimorgans two people share, the more likely they are to have a relatively recent common ancestor.

However, inheritance varies. Two people with similar relationships may share different amounts of DNA.

This is why DNA testing platforms normally show a range of possible relationships rather than one guaranteed answer.

Start With Your Closest Known Matches

Do not begin by examining hundreds of distant relatives.

Start with matches you already recognize, such as:

  • a parent;
  • a sibling;
  • an aunt or uncle;
  • a first cousin;
  • a known second cousin;
  • a grandparent.

Known relatives act as anchors.

When an unknown person also matches your known maternal cousin, that unknown match probably connects through your maternal family.

When another match appears alongside your father’s relatives, it is more likely to belong to your paternal side.

How to Tell Whether a DNA Match Is Maternal or Paternal

There are several practical methods.

1. Test a parent

The clearest method is to compare your matches with a biological parent.

A person who matches both you and your mother generally belongs to your maternal side.

A person who matches you but not your mother may belong to your paternal side, assuming the match is reliable and the platform displays the relevant comparison.

2. Use a known cousin

A known maternal or paternal cousin can also act as an anchor. Label the cousin first. Then examine the matches you share with that cousin.

3. Review shared matches

Most major DNA services include a shared-matches feature. Shared matches are people who match both you and the selected relative. A group of people who all match one another often belongs to the same family branch.

4. Compare surnames

Look for repeated maiden names and family surnames. Remember that surnames may be missing because:

  • women changed names after marriage;
  • people use private family trees;
  • the relationship passes through several female ancestors;
  • names changed after migration;
  • spellings changed between languages.

5. Compare locations

Locations are often more useful than surnames. Several matches whose ancestors lived in the same small town, parish or district may belong to the same branch even when their surnames differ.

Create Four Basic Family Groups

A useful first step is to divide matches according to your four grandparents:

  • Mother’s mother’s family.
  • Mother’s father’s family.
  • Father’s mother’s family.
  • Father’s father’s family.

Create a simple spreadsheet with these columns:

  • Match name
  • Shared DNA
  • Predicted relationship
  • Maternal or paternal
  • Likely grandparent branch
  • Shared matches
  • Surnames
  • Locations
  • Family-tree link
  • Contacted
  • Notes
  • Confidence level

Use a separate label or colour for every grandparent’s branch.

Do not mark a match as confirmed unless you have supporting evidence.

Suggested confidence labels:

  • Confirmed.
  • Highly likely.
  • Possible.
  • Unknown.
  • Contradictory evidence.

What Are Shared-Match Clusters?

A cluster is a group of DNA matches who also match one another.

Imagine that you match Anna, David and Sofia. Anna also matches David and Sofia. All three have ancestors from the same region. This suggests that the four of you may share an ancestral family line.

Clusters are especially useful when:

  • a match has no family tree;
  • surnames changed;
  • you do not know one side of your family;
  • your family migrated;
  • you are researching an adoption or unknown parent;
  • there are too many distant matches to examine separately.

A cluster does not automatically reveal the common ancestor. It narrows the search to a particular branch.

Why Do I Have So Many Distant DNA Matches?

Some users receive thousands of distant matches.

This can happen because:

  • many distant cousins have tested;
  • your ancestral population is well represented in the database;
  • relatives descend from large families;
  • cousins are related through more than one ancestral path;
  • a historically isolated community shares many ancestral connections;
  • the platform includes very small matches.

Do not try to identify every distant cousin.

Prioritize matches that have:

  • higher shared DNA;
  • useful family trees;
  • recognizable locations;
  • common shared matches;
  • repeated surnames;
  • clear ancestral records.

What If Different DNA Services Show Different Matches?

Different companies have different customer databases.

Your DNA has not changed, but the people available for comparison are different.

Platforms may also use different:

  • matching thresholds;
  • relationship estimates;
  • ethnicity models;
  • reference populations;
  • filtering rules;
  • family-tree tools.

A person will only appear as your match on a particular service if their DNA data is available in that service’s matching database.

How to Contact an Unknown DNA Match

Keep your first message short and respectful.

Do not begin with an accusation such as: “I think your father had a secret child.”

Use a neutral message:

Hi, we appear to share DNA and may have a family connection. I’m researching my family history and wondered whether you recognize the surnames or locations in my tree. I understand that unexpected DNA results can be sensitive, so there is no pressure to respond.

Include:

  • your first name;
  • the amount of shared DNA;
  • the family branch you are researching;
  • two or three relevant surnames;
  • important locations;
  • a respectful explanation of why you are contacting them.

Do not send several follow-up messages if the person does not respond.

Why Might a DNA Match Refuse Contact?

A match may not respond because:

  • they rarely use the account;
  • they tested only for ethnicity results;
  • they do not know their family history;
  • the result revealed something unexpected;
  • they are protecting another family member;
  • they are unsure how you are related;
  • they feel emotionally unprepared;
  • they do not want a relationship.

Silence does not necessarily mean rejection of you personally. It may reflect the other person’s own circumstances.

How to Build a Family Tree From DNA Matches

Start with documented facts.

For every person, record:

  • full name;
  • maiden name;
  • approximate dates;
  • birthplace;
  • marriage location;
  • residence;
  • death place;
  • parents;
  • children;
  • source of the information.

Then connect your DNA-match clusters to documented descendants of the same couples.

A responsible conclusion should look like:

“These three matches descend from different children of the same ancestral couple, making this branch the strongest current hypothesis.”

Not:

“The app says we are cousins, so this must be the right family.”

What to Do After Receiving Your DNA Results

Follow this order:

  1. Download or safely save your original results.
  2. Identify known relatives.
  3. Label maternal and paternal matches.
  4. Separate matches by grandparent branch.
  5. Examine the closest unknown matches.
  6. Review shared-match clusters.
  7. Build or update your family tree.
  8. Compare names and locations.
  9. Contact selected matches respectfully.
  10. Document what is confirmed and what remains uncertain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know which side of my family a DNA match comes from?

Compare the match with a tested parent, known cousin or other identified relative. Shared matches, surnames, locations and family trees can help place the person on the maternal or paternal side.

What does a high DNA match mean?

A larger amount of shared DNA usually suggests a closer biological relationship, but several relationships may fit the same range.

Why does my match have no familiar surnames?

The relationship may pass through married women, changed surnames, adoption, migration, incomplete family trees or several generations of separation.

Can a DNA match be wrong?

Matching systems can be less informative at very small amounts of shared DNA. Close matches generally deserve careful investigation, but the predicted relationship may still have several possible interpretations.

Should I contact every DNA match?

No. Begin with close matches and people whose family trees, locations or shared matches can help answer a specific question.

Can facial resemblance prove that someone is my relative?

No. Two people can look similar without being closely related, while genuine relatives may look very different.

Final Thoughts

A DNA-match list is not a collection of instant answers. It is a network of clues.

Begin with the closest known relatives, divide matches by family branch and investigate one group at a time.

You do not need to solve every match. Finding one correct shared ancestor can transform dozens of unknown names into a meaningful part of your family story.
