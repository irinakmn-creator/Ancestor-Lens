Why Did My Ancestry DNA Results Change? What an Ethnicity Update Really Means

Did your ancestry DNA percentages suddenly change? Learn why ethnicity estimates are updated, why regions disappear and what new results really mean.

Published: 2026-09-10 | Updated: 2026-09-10

Written by the Ancestor Lens Editorial Team. We provide carefully researched insights into DNA matching, genealogy, and visual heritage exploration.

You checked your ancestry results six months ago.

They said:

  • 38% Irish
  • 27% Germanic Europe
  • 21% Italian
  • 14% Scandinavian

Today you open the same account and see:

  • 31% Irish
  • 32% Germanic Europe
  • 25% Italian
  • 12% Northern European

You did not submit a new DNA sample.

Your DNA obviously did not change.

So why did your ancestry DNA results change?

The answer is that your DNA stayed the same, but the company's interpretation of it changed.

Ethnicity estimates are statistical models. As testing companies improve their databases, reference populations, algorithms and regional classifications, your percentages can be recalculated.

Did My DNA Actually Change?

No.

Your inherited DNA does not suddenly become more Italian, less Irish or more Scandinavian because a company updated its ancestry report.

What changed is the model used to interpret your genetic data.

A simplified ancestry-estimation process looks something like this:

  1. the company examines genetic markers in your DNA;
  2. it compares those markers with reference populations;
  3. an algorithm estimates which populations your DNA segments resemble;
  4. those similarities are translated into regions and percentages.

Change the reference data or the classification model and the final percentages can change too.

Why Do DNA Companies Update Ancestry Results?

Ancestry technology continues to evolve.

Testing services can improve:

  • reference populations;
  • sample sizes;
  • statistical models;
  • algorithms;
  • geographic boundaries;
  • confidence thresholds;
  • regional names;
  • population groupings.

An older system might classify a DNA segment as broadly “Southern European.”

A newer model might have enough information to classify the same signal more specifically.

Or the opposite might occur: what was once labeled one country may now be grouped with neighboring regions because the genetic signal overlaps.

1. Reference Populations Get Better

An ancestry test does not contain a genetic label saying “this piece is Italian.” Instead, companies compare your DNA with groups of people used as reference populations. As those datasets change, so can your estimate.

For example, a DNA segment previously associated most strongly with one region may later resemble a newly expanded neighboring reference population more closely.

Your DNA is identical. The comparison data changed.

2. Algorithms Improve

Companies regularly refine the mathematical methods used to classify DNA segments. A newer algorithm may distinguish similar populations more effectively, change confidence levels, identify smaller geographic groups, merge categories that were previously separated, or reconsider uncertain segments.

This can move percentages from one region to another.

3. Regional Categories Change

Human populations do not fit perfectly inside modern national borders. Genetic similarities frequently overlap across nearby regions.

This is particularly relevant in places with long histories of migration and shared ancestry. For example, genetic similarities may overlap across areas such as:

  • Ireland and Britain;
  • Germany and surrounding Central Europe;
  • Italy and the Mediterranean;
  • Greece and the Balkans;
  • Poland and Eastern Europe;
  • Spain and Portugal;
  • Scandinavia and Northern Europe.

A testing company may therefore reorganize or rename categories as its models improve.

Why Did One Ethnicity Disappear From My Results?

This is one of the most common sources of confusion.

Yesterday you had: 5% Scandinavian.

After an update: 0%.

Does that prove you have no Scandinavian ancestry?

Not necessarily.

A region can disappear because:

  • it represented a small low-confidence signal;
  • the DNA segment was reassigned;
  • a neighboring regional category became a better statistical match;
  • regional boundaries changed;
  • the company changed its threshold for displaying small estimates.

Small percentages are generally more likely to shift than large, stable regional patterns. Read our guide to learn more about what ancestry DNA percentages really mean.

Why Did a New Ethnicity Suddenly Appear?

The opposite can also happen.

An updated report may introduce a region that was never listed previously.

That does not mean new DNA appeared.

Instead, the service may now be able to interpret previously broad or uncertain segments differently.

For example, an older report might have used “Eastern Europe” while a newer model separates several more specific regional groups. Or a segment previously classified as “Broadly Southern European” may receive a more specific regional interpretation.

Why Did My Percentage Change So Much?

A shift of several percentage points can feel dramatic.

But ethnicity percentages should not be interpreted as exact measurements.

Imagine two neighboring populations have strong genetic overlap.

Version 1 of an algorithm assigns a set of DNA segments: 70% to Population A, 30% to Population B.

After improved reference data, Version 2 may interpret those same segments: 45% Population A, 55% Population B.

The genetic sequence being analyzed did not change. Its statistical classification did.

Does This Mean DNA Ancestry Tests Are Wrong?

Not necessarily.

It means ethnicity estimation has limits.

DNA testing is extremely useful for identifying genetic relationships and comparing DNA. But assigning every part of a genome to modern ethnicity labels is much more interpretive.

Human populations have migrated, mixed and changed throughout history. Modern national boundaries also do not map perfectly onto historical genetic populations.

This is why the word estimate matters. Use ancestry percentages as clues rather than absolute definitions of who you are.

Why Do Different Companies Give Different Results?

This is related — but it is a separate question.

Two DNA testing companies can produce different ancestry estimates because they use different reference groups, databases, algorithms, labels, and confidence models.

If your question is why Ancestry, MyHeritage, 23andMe or another company gives you different percentages, read our guide on why DNA results differ between companies.

This article focuses instead on why your results within the same service can change over time.

Can Small DNA Percentages Be Wrong?

Small percentages deserve extra caution.

A result such as 1%, 2%, or 3% may represent a real ancestry signal. But it can also be affected more strongly by statistical uncertainty, overlapping populations, algorithm changes, and reference-dataset changes.

Do not automatically build an entire family story around a 1% result. Look for supporting evidence such as documented ancestors, DNA matches, birthplaces, surnames, immigration records, and family stories.

What If My DNA Results Don't Match My Family Tree?

That can happen for several reasons:

  • Random inheritance: You do not inherit equal amounts of autosomal DNA from every distant ancestor.
  • Population overlap: Neighboring regions may share substantial genetic similarity.
  • Missing reference data: A company may have stronger reference populations for some regions than others.
  • Historical borders: Your ancestor may have lived in a place whose political borders and modern national identity changed repeatedly.
  • Incomplete family information: Family stories sometimes simplify complex migration histories.

The best approach is to compare DNA with historical evidence rather than expecting the ethnicity report to replace genealogy.

Why Can Siblings Get Different Results After the Same Update?

Because siblings do not inherit the same DNA.

Even full siblings receive different combinations of genetic segments from their parents. One sibling may carry more segments associated with one ancestry region than another.

That means an algorithm update can affect their reports differently. Read our detailed guide on why siblings can have different DNA results.

Does Your Face Change When Your DNA Estimate Changes?

Of course not.

But this raises an interesting question.

Many people compare the ancestry reported in their DNA results with what they think they look like. Someone might receive a large Irish result but constantly be told that they look Italian.

Visible appearance and DNA ethnicity percentages do not map directly onto each other. A face can reflect inherited traits without displaying your ancestry percentages proportionally. Learn more in our article: do I look like my DNA results.

What Should I Do After My Ancestry Results Change?

Do not immediately rewrite your entire family tree. Instead:

  1. Save your previous results: Screenshots can help you compare changes over time.
  2. Compare broad regions: Large regional patterns that remain consistent across updates may be more informative than small percentage changes.
  3. Look at DNA matches: Matches can provide more direct genealogical evidence than ethnicity percentages. Learn how to understand DNA matches.
  4. Compare with known relatives: Parents, grandparents and siblings can help determine which ancestry signals belong to which family branches.
  5. Review your family tree: Compare the updated estimate with documented locations and migration history using our guide on how to build a family tree.
  6. Treat small percentages cautiously: Especially if they appear and disappear between updates.
  7. Keep an open mind: Ancestry estimates will probably continue improving as reference data and methods evolve.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my ancestry DNA results suddenly change?

The testing company's reference populations, algorithms, confidence thresholds or regional categories were probably updated. Your DNA itself did not change.

Why did one ethnicity disappear from my DNA results?

A small or uncertain region may have been reassigned to another category after an update or may no longer meet the company's display threshold.

Why did a new region appear?

Improved data or a newer algorithm may interpret part of your existing DNA more specifically than before.

Can ethnicity percentages change again?

Yes. Ancestry estimates can continue to change as testing companies update their databases and methods.

Does a changed result mean the old result was fake?

No. The older result reflected the company's interpretation at that time. A newer model may interpret the same DNA differently.

Which ancestry result should I believe?

Use the newest result as the company's current estimate, but interpret it together with DNA matches, family records and genealogy rather than treating percentages as absolute facts.

Can my real ethnicity change?

Your family history does not change when an ancestry-testing algorithm changes. The displayed estimate is a statistical interpretation, not a change in your identity.

Final Thoughts

If your ancestry DNA results changed, remember the most important distinction:

Your DNA did not change. The interpretation changed.

Ethnicity estimates depend on evolving reference populations, algorithms, statistical models, regional categories, and confidence thresholds.

That is why a percentage can increase, decrease or disappear.

Use ancestry estimates as valuable clues. Then compare them with your DNA matches, documented family tree, historical records and family photographs. Those different forms of evidence together tell a much richer ancestry story than one percentage ever could.
