Autosomal DNA vs Y-DNA vs mtDNA: Which Test Should You Take?

The three main genealogy DNA tests do not answer the same question. Autosomal DNA surveys many recent family branches, Y-DNA follows one direct paternal line, and mitochondrial DNA follows one direct maternal line.

By Ancestor Lens Editorial TeamOctober 12, 202611 min read
Autosomal DNAY-DNAmtDNAGenetic genealogy

Quick answer

Choose an autosomal DNA test for cousin matches, ethnicity estimates and research across many branches of your family tree. Choose Y-DNA to investigate a direct father-to-son line; only a person with a Y chromosome can take the test, though anyone can use the results of a suitable male relative. Choose mtDNA to investigate a direct mother-to-child line; anyone can test, but only mothers pass mitochondrial DNA to the next generation.

The most useful test depends on the person and branch you are researching. A broad autosomal result cannot replace a targeted line test, while Y-DNA or mtDNA represents only one narrow path through a much larger family tree.

What is an autosomal DNA test?

Autosomal DNA tests examine markers across chromosomes 1–22. You inherit one copy of each autosome from your mother and one from your father, so the results contain a shuffled mixture from many family lines. This is the test type most people encounter in consumer ancestry services.

Autosomal tests are especially useful for finding people who share segments of DNA with you. A match list can support recent family-tree research, separate maternal and paternal clusters, identify unknown biological relatives and help test whether two documentary trees connect.

What autosomal DNA does well

  • Finds relatives from many branches rather than one direct line.
  • Supports research into parents, grandparents and more distant recent ancestors.
  • Provides the data used for consumer ethnicity estimates.
  • Works for anyone, regardless of sex.

Its reach is not unlimited. Recombination breaks inherited DNA into smaller pieces each generation, and a distant genealogical cousin may share no detectable segment with you. Read our guides to how far back ancestry DNA goes and how DNA inheritance changes by generation.

What is a Y-DNA test?

Y-DNA testing examines markers on the Y chromosome. A father generally passes his Y chromosome to his sons, making it useful for a direct paternal line: you, your father, his father, his father's father and onward. Because surnames in many cultures also followed paternal lines, Y-DNA projects sometimes use results to investigate surname groups. A genetic match does not by itself prove a particular surname or named ancestor.

Only a person with a Y chromosome can provide a Y-DNA sample. A woman researching her father's direct paternal line can ask an appropriate relative from that same unbroken line to test—for example, her father, brother with the same father, paternal uncle or a male-line cousin. The exact relative matters; a mother's brother follows a different paternal line.

One line, not “the male half” of your ancestry

Y-DNA follows a single chain of fathers. It does not represent every male ancestor. Your paternal grandmother's father, for example, is a male ancestor but is not on your direct Y-DNA line.

What is a mitochondrial DNA test?

Mitochondria contain a small amount of DNA separate from the chromosomes in the cell nucleus. People of all sexes inherit mitochondrial DNA from their mother. Mothers can pass it to their children, while fathers generally do not pass it onward.

That pattern follows one direct maternal chain: you, your mother, her mother, her mother's mother and so forth. mtDNA can support or challenge a hypothesis that two people descend through uninterrupted maternal lines from a shared female ancestor. It can also identify a maternal haplogroup associated with a much older branch of human migration.

A shared haplogroup is not proof of a recent relationship. Many people can belong to the same broad maternal branch, and mtDNA changes slowly. Documentary records are still needed to connect the result to named women in a family tree.

Which ancestry DNA test should you take?

If your goal is to find cousins or an unknown parent

Start with autosomal DNA. The broad match database is the main advantage. Close matches, shared matches and family trees are usually more informative than ethnicity percentages. Our DNA match guide explains how to group results before drawing conclusions.

If your question follows a direct paternal line

Use Y-DNA, ideally testing the person closest to the research question on the correct father-to-son line. Combine the result with birth, marriage, probate, land, military and surname evidence.

If your question follows a direct maternal line

Use mtDNA. Map the line carefully through mothers and look for records that reveal maiden names, because every marriage can create a surname change in the paper trail.

If you want a broad ethnicity estimate

Use an autosomal test, but treat regional percentages as estimates based on a company's reference populations and methods. Different services may produce different labels for overlapping populations. See how accurate ancestry DNA tests are before comparing percentages.

Can you combine autosomal, Y-DNA and mtDNA?

Yes. They answer complementary questions. An autosomal match may point to a branch, while Y-DNA or mtDNA can test whether the connection follows a specific unbroken line. The strongest conclusion combines genetic evidence with a sourced family tree and considers alternative relationships.

  1. Write one precise research question.
  2. Draw the inheritance path from the test taker to the target ancestor.
  3. Select the test that actually travels along that path.
  4. Choose the most informative living relative to test.
  5. Compare the DNA result with records and known relatives.

Limits, consent and privacy

DNA can reveal unexpected biological relationships. Before testing yourself or asking a relative, discuss what the test may uncover and obtain informed consent. Review the provider's current policies for sample storage, account deletion, relative matching, research participation, data downloads and law-enforcement access. Settings and policies can change, so read the terms at the time you test.

No test measures culture, nationality or personal identity. Genetic ancestry is one form of evidence. It should be interpreted with geography, migration, historical records and family knowledge rather than used as a complete definition of heritage.

Frequently asked questions

Is autosomal DNA the same as an ancestry DNA test?

Most broad consumer ancestry tests are autosomal tests. They may include ethnicity estimates and relative matching, but exact features depend on the provider.

Can a woman take a Y-DNA test?

A person needs a Y chromosome for a Y-DNA test. A woman can research a paternal line by testing an appropriate male relative who descends through the same uninterrupted father-to-son line.

Can men take a mitochondrial DNA test?

Yes. Everyone inherits mitochondrial DNA from their mother. Men can test it, although they generally do not pass it to their children.

Which DNA test goes back the farthest?

Y-DNA and mtDNA can follow single direct lines far into the past, while autosomal DNA covers many branches but becomes less reliable for identifying specific distant ancestors. “Farthest” and “broadest” are different goals.

Do I need all three tests?

No. Begin with a research question. Autosomal DNA is the usual starting point for general genealogy; add a line-specific test only when it can address a particular paternal or maternal hypothesis.
