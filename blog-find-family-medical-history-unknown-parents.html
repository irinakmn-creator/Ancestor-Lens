How to Find Your Family Medical History When You Do Not Know Your Biological Parents

Do not know your biological family’s medical history? Learn what information to collect, where to search, what to tell your doctor, and when to consider genetic counseling.

Medical forms often ask familiar questions:

  • Did your mother have cancer?
  • Did your father have heart disease?
  • Does diabetes run in your family?
  • Were any relatives diagnosed at a young age?

For someone who is adopted, donor-conceived, estranged from family or uncertain about biological parentage, the honest answer may be: “I do not know.”

Not knowing your biological family’s medical history can feel frightening. It may seem as though everyone else received an instruction manual that you were denied.

But incomplete family history does not mean that you cannot take care of your health.

You can document what you know, search for available information and work with a healthcare professional to make decisions based on your personal health, age, symptoms and available evidence.

The CDC advises people to collect information about conditions affecting close biological relatives, the age at diagnosis, and the age and cause of death. Even incomplete information can be useful when shared with a healthcare provider.

Why Family Medical History Matters

A family health history is a record of diseases and health conditions affecting biological relatives.

It can help a healthcare professional understand whether certain health problems appear repeatedly in a family.

Family history may influence discussions about:

  • screening;
  • prevention;
  • age to begin particular checks;
  • genetic counselling;
  • symptoms that deserve closer attention;
  • lifestyle risk factors;
  • pregnancy planning.

Family history includes more than genes. Relatives may also share environments, habits and other exposures that influence health.

Having a family history of a condition does not mean that you will definitely develop it. And having no known history does not guarantee that you will not.

Who Commonly Has an Unknown Family Health History?

You may lack biological family information if you are:

  • adopted;
  • donor-conceived;
  • raised by one parent;
  • estranged from relatives;
  • born after an anonymous pregnancy;
  • separated from family by war or migration;
  • uncertain about biological parentage;
  • descended from relatives whose records were destroyed;
  • part of a family where health was never discussed;
  • connected to relatives who refuse contact.

Unknown history is not a personal failure. It is a missing-data problem.

Tell Your Doctor That Your Family History Is Unknown

Do not leave the family-history section blank without explanation.

Write: Biological family medical history unknown.

Or: Limited information available from maternal side; paternal history unknown.

Tell the healthcare professional why the history is unavailable.

This distinction matters:

  • “No family history of heart disease” means you believe it did not occur.
  • “Family history unknown” means there is not enough information.

These are not the same clinical situation.

Start With Your Own Medical History

When family information is missing, your own health information becomes especially important.

Record:

  • current conditions;
  • previous diagnoses;
  • surgeries;
  • hospitalizations;
  • allergies;
  • medications;
  • unusual reactions;
  • recurring symptoms;
  • age when symptoms began;
  • blood pressure;
  • laboratory results;
  • pregnancy history;
  • lifestyle factors;
  • screening results.

Keep copies of important medical records and update the list regularly.

What Information Should You Try to Find?

You do not need a complete medical biography for every relative. Focus on:

  • major diagnoses;
  • age at diagnosis;
  • age and cause of death;
  • conditions affecting several relatives;
  • unusually early disease;
  • known inherited conditions;
  • pregnancy complications;
  • repeated miscarriages;
  • serious childhood conditions;
  • neurological or developmental conditions;
  • mental health conditions;
  • hearing or vision loss at an unusual age.

CDC recommends collecting information from both sides of the biological family, including parents, siblings, half-siblings, grandparents, aunts and uncles where possible.

Where to Search for Biological Family Medical Information

Availability depends on your country, age and legal circumstances. Possible sources include:

Adoption records

An adoption agency or government authority may hold non-identifying medical information supplied by biological parents. The completeness of these records varies greatly.

Birth records

A birth record may identify biological parents or provide information needed for further research. Access laws differ by jurisdiction.

Social service records

Child-welfare or social-service files may include family medical information, pregnancy history or notes about biological relatives.

Donor records

A clinic or donor program may hold health information collected at the time of donation. Ask whether updates are available and whether there is a process for reporting newly discovered hereditary conditions.

DNA matches

DNA relatives may help identify biological family branches. However, do not assume that an unknown match is willing or able to provide medical information.

Obituaries and death records

These may provide age and cause of death, although the information can be incomplete.

Relatives who are willing to communicate

A biological relative may agree to answer health questions without wanting an ongoing personal relationship. A limited information exchange can still be valuable.

How to Ask a Biological Relative About Medical History

Keep the request focused.

Example message:

I understand that you may not be comfortable establishing personal contact. I am trying to complete my medical history for my doctor. Would you be willing to share whether close biological relatives experienced major inherited conditions, cancer, heart disease, diabetes or serious illness at a young age? General information without names would still be very helpful.

Make clear that:

  • you are not demanding a relationship;
  • general information is acceptable;
  • they do not need to disclose names;
  • they can decline;
  • the information will be used for personal healthcare.

Create a Three-Generation Health Chart

When possible, organize information about:

First-degree relatives: biological parents, full siblings, half-siblings, biological children.

Second-degree relatives: grandparents, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, grandchildren.

More distant relatives: cousins, great-grandparents, great-aunts and great-uncles.

For each person, record:

  • Relationship
  • Maternal or paternal side
  • Major health conditions
  • Age at diagnosis
  • Age and cause of death
  • Source of information
  • Confirmed or unconfirmed
  • Date updated

The Surgeon General’s My Family Health Portrait is designed to help people record and organize family health information for sharing with relatives or healthcare providers.

What If the Information Is Only a Family Rumour?

Record it as unconfirmed.

For example: Maternal grandfather may have died from a heart-related condition in his early fifties. Exact diagnosis unknown.

Do not convert: “She had a female illness” into: “She had ovarian cancer.”

Older generations may have used vague language for cancer, mental illness, infertility, pregnancy complications and other conditions. Use the original wording until documentation confirms more.

Should You Take a Consumer DNA Test for Medical Information?

An ancestry test and a medical genetic test are not automatically the same thing.

Consumer DNA results may:

  • provide ancestry estimates;
  • identify relatives;
  • report selected genetic variants;
  • generate risk-style information.

They do not necessarily provide a complete assessment of inherited medical risk. Do not make medical decisions based only on an ancestry app, facial-analysis app or unconfirmed consumer result. Discuss health concerns with a qualified healthcare professional.

When to Consider Genetic Counselling

A genetic counsellor can help evaluate:

  • your personal medical history;
  • the limited family information available;
  • whether genetic testing may be useful;
  • what a result can and cannot reveal;
  • possible effects on biological relatives;
  • privacy and emotional considerations.

Genetic counselling may be especially worth discussing when:

  • you have an unusually early diagnosis;
  • the same rare condition appears in known relatives;
  • several related cancers are reported;
  • you are planning pregnancy and have specific concerns;
  • a clinician suspects an inherited condition;
  • a biological relative reports a known pathogenic variant.

Can Your Ethnicity Result Replace Medical Family History?

No. An ethnicity estimate cannot tell you that you personally have or will develop a disease.

A broad regional result is not a substitute for medical screening, personal health history, clinical genetic testing, a diagnosis, or consultation with a professional.

People within every ancestry group are medically diverse. Avoid assuming that an ancestry percentage automatically determines your individual health risks.

What You Can Do When No Records Are Available

Even when the biological family history remains completely unknown, you can still:

  1. Tell healthcare providers that the history is unavailable.
  2. Attend routine medical appointments.
  3. Follow age-appropriate screening advice.
  4. Keep a detailed personal health record.
  5. Report new or unusual symptoms.
  6. Ask whether your personal history changes screening needs.
  7. Avoid unverified medical conclusions from ancestry results.
  8. Update your records whenever new information appears.
  9. Keep documents in a format you can share.
  10. Seek professional genetic advice for specific concerns.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I write when I do not know my family medical history?

Write “biological family medical history unknown” or clearly describe which family side is unknown. Do not write “no family history” unless you actually know that the condition did not occur.

Can an adopted person access biological family medical records?

Access depends on local law, the type of adoption and the records held by agencies or authorities. Some people may receive non-identifying health information even when identifying details remain restricted.

Can DNA matches help me find medical history?

DNA matches may help identify biological relatives, but medical information should be requested respectfully and verified when possible.

Can a doctor help without family history?

Yes. A healthcare professional can use your age, symptoms, personal medical history, screening results and other risk factors even when family history is unavailable.

Should I take a genetic test because my family history is unknown?

Not automatically. Discuss the specific concern with a healthcare professional or genetic counsellor to determine whether testing is appropriate.

Does ancestry reveal my risk of disease?

Ancestry may sometimes form part of a broader medical assessment, but an ethnicity estimate alone cannot determine individual disease risk.

Can a facial ancestry app detect genetic conditions?

No. A photo-based ancestry app cannot diagnose inherited medical conditions or replace clinical genetic testing.

Final Thoughts

Not knowing your biological family’s medical history can create uncertainty, but it does not make you powerless.

Document what you know. Clearly mark what is missing. Ask focused questions. Save every reliable piece of information. Share the result with a healthcare professional rather than trying to interpret it alone.

You may never reconstruct every part of your biological family’s medical history. But even an incomplete record can become more useful when it is accurate, organised and updated over time.
