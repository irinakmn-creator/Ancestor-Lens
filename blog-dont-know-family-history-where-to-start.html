I Don’t Know My Family History: 7 Ways to Start Exploring Your Roots

Don’t know much about your family history? Start exploring your roots with seven simple steps, from family stories to photos, records and DNA testing.

Maybe you know where your parents were born but little about the generations before them. Maybe your family moved between countries, names changed over time, or the stories simply weren’t passed down.

If you don’t know much about your family history, you don’t need to have every answer before you begin. Start with what you know, record where it came from, and follow one question at a time.

Here are seven practical ways to get started today.

1. Write Down What You Already Know

Begin with yourself and work backward generation by generation.

Make a simple list of your parents, grandparents, and any other relatives you know. For each person, write down:

  • Full name and any known maiden names, nicknames, or spelling variations
  • Approximate birth, marriage, and death dates
  • Place of birth, residence, or burial
  • Names of parents, siblings, spouses, or close family friends
  • Languages spoken at home and religious affiliations

It’s completely fine to leave spaces blank. The goal is to see clearly what you already know and identify where your questions begin.

2. Ask Questions While Stories Are Still Available

A relative may not know the full family history, but they might remember crucial details that never made it onto an official record.

Instead of asking broadly, “Where is our family from?”, try targeted questions:

  • Where did your grandparents live when you were a child?
  • Did anyone in the family emigrate or serve in the military?
  • What languages or dialects did older relatives speak at holidays?
  • Was our surname ever pronounced or spelled differently?
  • Who is the oldest ancestor anyone in the family ever talked about?

Record who told you each story. Oral memories offer invaluable leads, but details should eventually be verified against archival records.

3. Look Through Photographs and Documents

An old envelope, a naturalization certificate, an obituary clipping, or a family photo album may contain a town name or maiden name you’ve never heard before.

Examine both sides of old photographs. Check for handwritten notes, studio embossed marks, and postal cancellation stamps. If you discover unidentified faces, our guide to identifying people in old family photos offers a step-by-step framework to investigate.

4. Choose One Family Line to Research First

Genealogy research becomes much easier and far less overwhelming when you narrow your focus.

Instead of trying to discover where all eight great-grandparents originated simultaneously, start with one specific question:

“Where was my maternal grandmother born?”

Search specifically for evidence that answers that single question. Once you have a verified certificate or census entry, move to her parents.

5. Search Historical Records

Depending on the country and time period, key genealogical records include:

  • Birth, marriage, and death certificates
  • Federal and state census returns
  • Passenger arrival lists and immigration manifests
  • Church baptismal and marriage registries
  • Newspaper announcements and local community directories
  • Military draft registration cards

Remember that surnames, town names, and national borders changed frequently over the past two centuries. Always test phonetic spelling variants.

6. Decide Whether a DNA Test Is Right for You

A commercial DNA ancestry test analyzes autosomal markers against reference populations to estimate continental and regional origins. Some services also match you with distant genetic cousins who share common ancestors.

Keep limitations in mind: reference datasets differ across testing companies, meaning percentages vary between providers. Read more about how far back ancestry DNA goes and review provider privacy terms before submitting a biological sample.

7. Explore Family Resemblance for Curiosity

Sometimes a physical family resemblance is the very spark that ignites your curiosity about the past.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I start researching my family history with zero information?

Start with yourself and work backward. Write down full names, birthplaces, approximate dates, and known locations for your parents and grandparents. Even partial details provide clues for historical record searches.

Should I take a DNA test first or search genealogy records?

Starting with family conversations, documents, and archival records is usually recommended because DNA test results require family trees and documented paper trails to make sense of genetic matches.

What free records can I search to find my ancestors?

Federal and state censuses, national archives, immigration passenger arrival lists, historical newspaper archives, church registries, and public cemetery databases frequently provide free access to ancestor records.

Can an AI photo app build my family tree?

No. Photo-based AI apps like Ancestor Lens generate visual heritage interpretations and historical look-alikes for entertainment and curiosity. They do not access genealogical archives or verify ancestral relationships.
